As the semester moved forward, the first major academic checkpoint arrived: mid-term exams. Before the actual mids, we had a Programming Fundamentals quiz that gave us a rough sense of what to expect in terms of paper pattern and difficulty. I walked into that quiz with a reasonable idea of the format, which made me feel slightly more prepared going into the real thing.

That preparation didn't quite translate the way I expected. When I sat down for the actual mid-term paper, the results felt completely different from what the quiz had suggested. Dr. Bilal had clearly designed the mid-term to be tougher than the quiz, and at the time, it genuinely caught a lot of us off guard. Looking back, I think part of the difficulty was simply that this was our very first mid-term experience as university students. We didn't yet have a reference point for how exams here compared to anything we'd encountered before, so naturally, it felt more intimidating than it might have otherwise.

What stood out to me afterward, though, was that the paper actually did reflect what we had been taught. Dr. Bilal had built our foundation carefully throughout the semester, and the exam questions, while phrased differently and pushed to a slightly higher level of difficulty than we expected, were still rooted in those fundamentals. The challenge wasn't that the material was foreign. It was that the questioning style asked us to apply what we knew rather than simply recall it, and that shift in expectation was new for all of us. In hindsight, I think the bigger factor at play was simply inexperience: this was the first mid-term paper of our university lives, and that alone made it feel harder than it might have been for someone more used to the format.

My own marks on that mid-term landed somewhere around average. Not a disaster, but not the result I was hoping for either. Still, I walked away from it with a clear sense of what I needed to do differently, and a genuine belief that I could close that gap by the time finals came around. That kind of self-assessment, recognizing where you stand without spiraling about it, turned out to be one of the more useful skills I picked up that semester.

Alongside the mid-term stress, our Programming Fundamentals labs were running in parallel, with their own evaluations happening at the same time. I made sure to stay on top of these, since Dr. Bilal had a particular approach to grading lab work that I genuinely appreciated. He evaluated us on merit rather than simply checking whether code ran correctly. That meant being asked to explain our code and walk through our logic, which forced a deeper level of understanding than just getting an output to match an expected result. It also meant the evaluation process itself was more rigorous, since explaining your reasoning out loud exposes gaps in understanding that a working program alone might hide.

Between the mid-term wake-up call and the ongoing lab evaluations, this stretch of the semester taught me something that would matter for the rest of my degree: real understanding shows up under pressure, and the only way to build that kind of understanding is to engage with the material rather than just complete it.

Reflecting on it further, I think there's a broader lesson here about expectations versus reality that applies well beyond just this one exam. Going into any new academic format, whether it's your first university mid-term, your first lab evaluation, or even your first group project, you tend to build a mental model of what to expect based on whatever limited information you have available. For me, that quiz beforehand had set an expectation, and the actual exam violated that expectation by being noticeably harder. The frustration in that moment wasn't really about the difficulty of the questions themselves. It was about the mismatch between what I'd prepared for and what I actually encountered.

What helped me move past that frustration fairly quickly was talking to a few classmates after the exam and realizing that almost everyone had felt the same gap between the quiz and the actual paper. That shared reaction told me something useful: this wasn't a personal failure to prepare adequately. It was a structural mismatch in how the quiz and the mid-term had been calibrated relative to each other, something that affected the whole class roughly equally rather than singling anyone out. Recognizing that distinction, between a personal shortfall and a shared, structural surprise, made it a lot easier to approach the rest of the semester with a clearer head rather than carrying unnecessary self-doubt into the next assessment.

I also started paying closer attention, from this point onward, to exactly how Dr. Bilal phrased and structured questions during lectures and lab sessions, since that mid-term experience had taught me that the way a concept gets tested can differ meaningfully from the way it gets explained. That small shift in how I studied, paying attention not just to the content of what was being taught but to the style of questioning used around it, ended up being useful preparation for the final exam and for every assessment that came after it during my time in this program.

In a strange way, that first mid-term, despite the average result it produced, turned out to be one of the more useful early lessons of the semester. It recalibrated my expectations for what university assessments would actually demand, and it gave me a concrete target to improve against heading into finals, rather than just a vague sense that I should try harder next time.

It's a small thing, but I still think about how differently that mid-term experience could have gone if I'd taken the lower-than-hoped-for marks as proof that I wasn't cut out for the program, rather than as a calibration problem to learn from. The difference between those two interpretations of the same result is enormous, and choosing the second one made the rest of the semester feel like a series of solvable problems rather than a slow confirmation of self-doubt.
