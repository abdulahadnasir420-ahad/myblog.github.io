If I had to pick one project from my entire second semester that took me through nearly every stage of actually building something real, it would be the first DBMS project. Looking back at it now, what makes it stand out is not any single difficult moment, but how many different stages it actually had, from the very first step of choosing a dataset all the way to standing in front of people explaining what we had built.

The project started with something that sounds simple on paper but actually required real thought: selecting a dataset from an authentic, reliable source. We did not just want to pick something convenient. We wanted data that was credible enough to design a proper database around, since everything that followed in the project depended on having that foundation solid from the start. Once we had settled on a dataset we trusted, we moved into designing the actual database structure around it, which meant translating raw data into something organized, relational, and usable, the kind of practical database design work that lecture theory alone does not fully prepare you for until you actually do it yourself.

After the database itself was in place, we moved into training a model on top of it. This is where the project stopped being purely a database exercise and started blending into something closer to applied machine learning, taking the structured data we had organized and using it to actually train a model rather than just store and query information. It was a noticeably different kind of work than anything we had done in regular coursework up to that point, and it forced us to think about our database design not just in terms of organization, but in terms of how well it could actually feed into a working model afterward.

What made this particular project feel different from earlier work, though, was the addition of FastAPI. This time, we were expected to build an actual frontend for the project using FastAPI, rather than just presenting backend logic or raw database queries. That meant the project was not finished once the model was trained. We still had to design something that could actually present and interact with the work we had done, which added a whole additional layer of design thinking on top of the database and modeling work we had already completed.

And we did not stop there. Beyond just the one dataset and model, we ended up working with different sets of data throughout the project, expanding the scope of what we were doing rather than keeping it narrow. This meant more testing, more adjusting of our database structure to accommodate different kinds of input, and more time spent making sure everything still worked correctly as the scope grew.

By the time the project was ready, it was time for the exhibition, and much like the Programming Fundamentals exhibition from my first semester, this one followed a similar pattern. People were invited to come see what we had built, and we had to be ready to explain and demonstrate our work in front of an audience rather than just submit it quietly. Honestly, despite all the work that had gone into getting there, the exhibition itself ended up being a genuinely good experience. We spent a good amount of time there, talking through our project, watching how people reacted to what we had built, and feeling, for at least that day, like all the earlier database design, model training, and frontend work had actually amounted to something worth showing.
