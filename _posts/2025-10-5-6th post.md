For our Programming Fundamentals final project, Dr. Bilal provided the class with a set of datasets and asked each group to train different models against them. This was the first time I was applying everything we'd learned about machine learning, data loading, visualization, and the classification-versus-regression distinction, to an actual, self-contained piece of work rather than a classroom exercise.

My group ended up working with a dataset sourced from PakWheels, Pakistan's well-known platform for buying and selling cars. The specific problem we tackled was predicting the price of new company cars based on the patterns in that dataset. It's the kind of project that sounds simple in one sentence but actually requires working through a real pipeline: cleaning and understanding the data, choosing the right kind of model for a price-prediction problem, training it, and then evaluating whether the predictions actually held up against real-world numbers.

The project turned out to be genuinely successful, and I mean that in a very concrete sense. The model's results were solid, and when it came time to present our work, the presentation itself went well too. A lot of that success came down to consistent support from my group members throughout the process. Projects like this rarely come together because of one person doing everything; they come together because a group manages to combine effort and cover each other's gaps, and that's exactly what happened here.

Looking back, this project mattered for more than just the grade attached to it. It was the first time I experienced the full arc of a data-driven project: starting with raw, unprocessed information, working through the uncertainty of not knowing exactly how the model would perform, and arriving at a result that actually made sense and could be explained clearly to other people. That sense of closure, of seeing a model you trained actually produce something usable, is hard to replicate with theory alone.

Working through this project also taught me a few things that no amount of theoretical study could have covered on its own. Real datasets, even ones as seemingly straightforward as car listings, are rarely clean by default. There are missing values, inconsistent formatting, and entries that simply don't make sense without some digging. Before we could train anything, we had to spend real time just understanding what we were working with: which columns actually mattered for predicting price, which ones were noise, and how to handle gaps in the data without distorting the results. That cleaning and exploration phase ended up taking nearly as much effort as the model training itself, which was a bit of a surprise the first time through.

Choosing the right approach for the prediction problem was its own small decision point. Since we were predicting a continuous value, the price of a car, rather than sorting cars into discrete categories, this was a textbook regression problem, and recognizing that connected directly back to the classification-versus-regression distinction we'd just learned. Being able to actually apply a concept we'd only recently learned, rather than just defining it on a quiz, made the whole exercise feel a lot more real.

Group dynamics played a bigger role in this project than I expected going in. Splitting up work like data cleaning, model training, and presentation preparation meant that the final result depended on everyone pulling their weight, and thankfully, that's exactly what happened. There's a particular kind of relief that comes from realizing your group members are just as invested in the outcome as you are, especially on a first major project where nobody has a clear sense yet of how reliable their teammates will turn out to be.

The presentation itself was a useful experience in its own right. Explaining a trained model to an audience, walking through why we chose the dataset we did, how we processed it, what model we trained, and why the results made sense, forced a different kind of understanding than just getting the code to run. You can sometimes get a model to produce reasonable output without fully grasping why it works. Standing in front of people and being asked to explain your reasoning closes that gap quickly.

Dr. Bilal's broader approach to assigning datasets throughout the course leaned toward problems with genuine real-world weight. Datasets like the one we used from PakWheels carry actual market value and reflect real consumer behavior, which made the project feel less like an artificial classroom assignment and more like practice for the kind of problems we'd eventually encounter outside university. That framing, of treating student projects as small-scale versions of real industry problems, is something I came to appreciate more as the semester went on.

This project also marked a personal milestone for me. It was the first time I could point to something tangible and say, "I built this, and it actually works." For a first-semester engineering student still getting used to university life, that feeling carried a lot of weight, and it gave me a template I'd return to for every project that came after it: understand the data first, choose the right approach deliberately, lean on your group, and make sure you can explain your own work clearly when it matters.

Months later, when Dr. Bilal announced our next major project, a model that needed its own designed dataset connected to a real FastAPI front end, I found myself drawing directly on the habits this first project had built. The instinct to inspect data before trusting it, the discipline of matching the model type to the actual shape of the problem, and the willingness to lean on group members rather than trying to carry an entire project alone, all of it traced back to those weeks spent wrestling with a spreadsheet of used car listings from PakWheels.
