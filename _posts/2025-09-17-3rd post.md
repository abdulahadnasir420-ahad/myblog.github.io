With my FSc result settled and my confidence growing, university started to feel less like an obstacle course and more like a place I actually wanted to invest myself in. I began picking up the basics more naturally, and around this time, an opportunity came up that I hadn't planned for at all: becoming Class Representative.

The backstory of how I ended up taking on this role is honestly a bit of an unusual one. Every class needs a CR to help manage day-to-day coordination, but when the question came up among us, nobody really wanted to step forward. I was one of the people who didn't want the responsibility either, mostly because of one specific concern. Our Calculus instructor at the time was known for being quite strict, and the thought of being the point of contact between a strict teacher and an entire class felt like more pressure than I wanted to take on as a brand-new student still finding his footing.

That hesitation might have stuck if not for one of my closest friends in the program, someone I'll refer to here simply as GR, who genuinely insisted that I should take it on. She didn't just suggest it casually. She promised that she would support me through whatever came with the role, no matter what. There's something about having someone you trust make that kind of commitment that shifts your own thinking. If she believed in this enough to push for it and back it with her support, I figured it was worth trusting her judgment over my own hesitation. So I agreed, and looking back, it turned out to be one of the better decisions of that semester.

The early days of being CR weren't smooth. There were plenty of small difficulties: coordinating between students and faculty, making sure announcements actually reached everyone, handling the inevitable friction that comes whenever someone holds even a small position of responsibility. But like most things in that first semester, it got easier with time. The system around me started clicking into place, and gradually, my work as CR began to actually earn the class's appreciation rather than just being tolerated.

There's one specific moment from this period that stuck with me. One of our professors once made a comment that reframed how I thought about the entire role. He said that titles like CR and GR are just labels, just names assigned to a position. What actually defines a good person is whether they help others when it matters, regardless of what title they're holding at the time. That single idea changed how I approached the responsibility. It wasn't about being "the CR." It was about being someone the class could rely on, with or without the title attached to my name.

Taking on this role also meant building a stronger working relationship with Dr. Bilal, since coordinating Programming Fundamentals logistics for the whole class often ran through me. He trusted that I'd handle the responsibility properly, and that trust pushed me to actually live up to it rather than just go through the motions.

By the end of this stretch, I had three things going for me that I hadn't had at the start of the semester: a clearer understanding of how university actually worked, a leadership role I'd grown into rather than sought out, and a friend whose insistence had nudged me toward something genuinely worthwhile. None of this was part of any plan. It just happened, the way a lot of meaningful things in university tend to.

In hindsight, I think the most valuable part of taking on the CR role wasn't any single task I completed as part of it. It was the shift in how I related to the rest of my class. Before becoming CR, my interactions with classmates were mostly limited to whoever I happened to sit near or whoever I'd been paired with for a lab assignment. Once I took on the role, I had a reason to talk to nearly everyone, to understand what different students were struggling with, and to act as a kind of bridge between the class as a whole and the faculty teaching us. That broader visibility into how an entire class experiences a semester, not just my own narrow slice of it, gave me a perspective I wouldn't have had otherwise.

There were also moments where the role tested my patience in ways I hadn't expected. Coordinating something as simple as collecting assignment submissions on time, multiplied across thirty or forty students with their own schedules and excuses, turns out to be a genuinely difficult logistical exercise. Not because any individual task was hard, but because the sheer number of small moving parts meant something was always slightly behind schedule. Learning to chase down missing submissions without becoming a nag, and learning to communicate deadlines clearly enough that people couldn't claim confusion later, were skills I picked up entirely through trial and error rather than anything taught in a classroom.

The relationship with GR also deepened through this period in a way that felt natural rather than forced. Having someone who had pushed me into the role in the first place, and who genuinely followed through on her promise to help carry the weight of it, meant that even the harder weeks of CR work never felt entirely like a solo burden. We'd split tasks, cover for each other when one of us was swamped with other coursework, and generally treat the responsibility as something we were managing together rather than something I'd been individually saddled with. That kind of partnership, I'd argue, is often the actual difference between a role feeling sustainable and a role feeling like a slow drain on your time and energy.

Looking back at this entire stretch now, from the initial reluctance to take on the role, through the early friction with coordinating a strict Calculus instructor's expectations, to the eventual point where the work started earning genuine appreciation from the class, I can see a pattern that would repeat itself again and again throughout my degree: the things I was most hesitant to take on often turned out to be the ones that taught me the most.
