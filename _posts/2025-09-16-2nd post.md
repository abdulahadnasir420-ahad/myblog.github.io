Once classes properly began, I learned that the core subject of my first semester was Programming Fundamentals, and it was being taught by Dr. Bilal Ahmad. I didn't know it yet, but this one detail would end up shaping almost the entire rhythm of my first two semesters at UET Faisalabad. Dr. Bilal's approach to teaching wasn't about getting students to memorize syntax or copy code. His focus was on building real understanding, the kind where a concept actually clicks rather than just being recited back on an exam paper. Every topic he introduced, he broke down deeply, layer by layer, until it made sense from the ground up rather than from the surface.

That style of teaching mattered a lot in those early weeks, because everything else around me was still unfamiliar. My hostel life meant adjusting to mess food, which, however you look at it, is simply not comparable to a home-cooked meal. There was no real competition there at all. But this is where the human side of university life started to show itself. Seniors at the hostel noticed that things felt difficult for someone new, and instead of brushing it off, they took the time to encourage me. Their message was simple: things feel hard right now, but give it time, and this will become one of the better memories of your life. It was the kind of reassurance that costs nothing to give but means everything to receive.

It wasn't just the seniors, either. Teachers, Dr. Bilal included, played a real role in motivating students through that adjustment period. Having an instructor who takes the time to explain not just programming concepts but also the bigger picture, who genuinely wants you to settle in and succeed, makes an enormous difference when you're trying to find your footing in a brand-new environment. Slowly, the system started to make sense. I began understanding how the semester worked, how labs were structured, and how to actually keep pace with the coursework.

There was, however, one looming question that I couldn't shake during this period: my FSc result. Computer Engineering at UET had a minimum eligibility threshold, and if a student's overall marks fell below 60%, continuing the degree simply wasn't an option. That uncertainty sat with me for weeks. I remember the exact moment the result was announced. We were actually in the Programming Fundamentals lab when it happened, and Dr. Bilal told the class we could go ahead and check our results on the lab computers right then and there. The second I saw mine, a wave of relief washed over me. I had cleared the threshold, and the degree was officially still mine to pursue. I left that lab and went straight home, and it remains one of the best days of that entire semester.

That FSc result moment was a turning point. With the eligibility question settled, I could finally stop worrying about whether I belonged in this program and start actually engaging with it. The next chapter of my hostel and academic life began here, fueled by a renewed sense of confidence and a professor whose teaching style made even an intimidating subject feel approachable.

Looking back, I think the relief of that result day was magnified by exactly how much uncertainty had been sitting underneath the surface for weeks beforehand. It's one thing to be nervous about adjusting to hostel life or finding your way around a new campus. It's another thing entirely to be nervous about whether you'll even be allowed to continue the degree you've already started attending classes for. That particular kind of anxiety, the threat of being retroactively disqualified from something you'd already begun building a life around, isn't something that gets easier just because you keep yourself busy with coursework in the meantime. It sits quietly in the background of everything else you're doing, and it only fully lifts once the actual result is in front of you.

What made that day even more memorable was the setting. Finding out in a Programming Fundamentals lab, surrounded by classmates checking their own results at the same moment, gave the whole experience a shared quality rather than an isolated one. Some students around me were relieved in the same way I was. A few, I'm sure, weren't as fortunate, and I think about that sometimes, how the same announcement that gave me a green light to continue must have landed very differently for someone else in that same room. It's a reminder that even shared institutional moments carry very personal weight depending on where you're standing.

Dr. Bilal's role in that moment, simply telling the class to go ahead and check, rather than making it into a bigger production than it needed to be, fit the pattern I'd already started noticing in how he ran his classroom. He didn't dramatize things that didn't need drama, and he didn't downplay things that genuinely mattered either. That balance, I think, is part of what made both his teaching and his presence as an instructor feel trustworthy from early on, and it's a big part of why I wanted to properly credit him before moving further into the rest of the semester's story.

I want to take a moment in this post to properly credit the person who shaped so much of this early journey: Dr. Bilal Ahmad. Beyond Programming Fundamentals, Dr. Bilal would go on to teach my Database Systems course as well in the following semester, and his background working with AI and machine learning model training gave both courses a depth that went well beyond textbook material. For anyone interested in following his work or research, you can find him on [LinkedIn](https://www.linkedin.com/in/drbilalphd/), [Google Scholar](https://scholar.google.com.au/citations?user=8nZ0jVkAAAAJ&hl=en), and [Facebook](https://www.facebook.com/Dr.BilalAhm).
