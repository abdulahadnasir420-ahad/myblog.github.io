Once the second mid-term exams were behind us, Dr. Bilal sat the class down and walked us through what was coming next in detail: two major projects that would define the remainder of the semester. Unlike the more incremental assignments we'd handled up to this point, both of these felt like a genuine step up in scope and ambition.

The first project builds directly on what we started with the PakWheels car price prediction model back in Programming Fundamentals, but with a meaningfully expanded scope this time around. Instead of simply training a model on a dataset that was handed to us, we're now expected to design the dataset ourselves as part of the project. On top of that, the model needs to be connected to FastAPI, meaning we're also responsible for building an actual front end around it. This is a significant leap from where we started. The PakWheels project was about proving we could train a model that produced sensible predictions. This new project asks us to think about the entire pipeline: how the data is structured from the ground up, how the model is trained against it, and how an actual user would interact with that model through a working interface. It's the difference between writing a script that works and building something that resembles an actual deployable application.

The second project is the one you're reading the result of right now: a portfolio project built around GitHub. The idea is to create a repository that documents our journey through the semester, written up as a series of posts that capture the actual experience of going through this Computer Engineering program rather than just listing finished assignments. Dr. Bilal's reasoning behind this assignment, as I understood it, was about building a digital footprint as a student, a body of writing and reflection that exists publicly and tells a more complete story than a transcript or a resume line ever could. That's exactly what this series of posts has been: an attempt to take notes I kept over the semester and turn them into something coherent, something that connects the hostel adjustment, the CR responsibilities, the PakWheels project, the exhibition, and the shift into Database Systems into one continuous narrative.

Sitting down to actually plan this GitHub portfolio made me realize how much had actually happened over the past two semesters. What started as a nervous first day at an unfamiliar campus had turned into a fairly full record of experiences: adjusting to hostel life, learning under an instructor who pushed for real understanding rather than surface-level completion, taking on a leadership role I hadn't planned for, building and presenting an actual machine learning project, organizing an exhibition on a single day's notice, and now stepping into a more advanced project that combines model training with real application development.

Both of these projects, the FastAPI-connected model and this portfolio series itself, feel like a fitting way to close out this stretch of the program. One is a technical leap forward, pushing past where Programming Fundamentals left off into something closer to a real-world deployable system. The other is a reflective one, a chance to actually sit with everything that's happened since that first nervous day on campus and put it into words.

There's more to come as both of these projects move forward, the dataset design and FastAPI integration on one side, and whatever final reflections this portfolio series ends up holding on the other. For now, this marks the point where two semesters' worth of notes finally caught up to the present.

Putting this whole series together, going back through scattered notes from a first nervous day on campus all the way through a second mid-term taken under disrupted Ramadan conditions, gave me a different kind of appreciation for how much actually changes over the course of just two semesters. The version of myself who walked nervously into that first orientation session genuinely didn't know what a university lab evaluation looked like, had never coordinated a class-wide submission, and had no real concept of what it meant to train a machine learning model. The version of myself writing this post has done all of that, more than once, and is now being asked to take the next step into a more advanced project that combines model design with actual application development.

I think that's the real value in an exercise like this GitHub portfolio. It's easy to lose track of incremental growth when you're living through it day by day, since each individual week rarely feels like a dramatic leap forward. It's only when you sit down and actually write out the full arc, from the FSc result anxiety to the PakWheels project to the exhibition to the Ramadan-disrupted mid-term, that the cumulative distance covered becomes obvious. A single semester rarely feels transformative while you're inside it. Two semesters, written out and read back in sequence, clearly are.

There's also something worth saying about the role Dr. Bilal's teaching played across this entire stretch, beyond just the individual courses themselves. The consistency of his approach, starting from fundamentals, building up methodically, and pushing students toward real understanding rather than surface-level completion, is the thread that ties Programming Fundamentals and Database Systems together despite how different the two subjects are on the surface. That consistency gave the past two semesters a kind of structural backbone that made it easier to trust the process even during the harder stretches, the difficult mid-term, the online classes with poor audio, the tight exhibition deadline.

As this new project gets underway, designing a dataset from scratch and connecting a trained model to an actual FastAPI front end, I expect the same pattern to repeat: an initial period of unfamiliarity and difficulty, followed by gradual understanding, followed eventually by something I can look back on the way I now look back on the PakWheels project, as proof that the harder version of a task is usually worth pushing through.
