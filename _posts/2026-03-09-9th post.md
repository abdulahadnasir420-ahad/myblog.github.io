With my first semester behind me, the second semester arrived, and it came with a noticeably different atmosphere. This one was a summer semester, which meant the weather was considerably hotter than anything I'd experienced during the first term. It also happened to overlap with a cluster of major events on the calendar: Ramadan, Eid-ul-Fitr, and later Eid-ul-Adha, all falling within the same academic stretch. Balancing coursework against that kind of calendar is its own particular challenge, since the rhythm of daily life shifts considerably during Ramadan in particular.

Academically, this semester's core subject was Database Systems, and once again, the course was being taught by Dr. Bilal. Having already gone through Programming Fundamentals under his guidance, I had a reasonably strong expectation of what to expect this time around: a methodical, basics-first approach that built understanding layer by layer rather than rushing through material. That expectation turned out to be accurate. Dr. Bilal carried the same teaching philosophy into Database Systems that had worked so well in Programming Fundamentals, starting from fundamentals and building up steadily.

The early weeks of the course covered the practical tools of the trade: WordPress, WAMP Server, and MySQL Workbench were introduced first, giving us a working environment to actually practice what we were learning. From there, we moved into SQL queries, again starting from the basics before increasing in complexity. Entity-Relationship modeling was also folded into the mid-term syllabus, and as had been the case with Programming Fundamentals, this structured progression meant our understanding kept building steadily rather than hitting sudden gaps.

What stood out to me about this semester, even early on, was how Database Systems is genuinely a different kind of subject from Programming Fundamentals. Where PF was about logic, syntax, and eventually machine learning model training, Database Systems is about structuring and managing information itself: how data is modeled, stored, queried, and kept consistent. Dr. Bilal's strength as an instructor for both Programming Fundamentals and Database Systems lies in how he frames each subject's basics clearly enough that the underlying logic becomes intuitive rather than something to memorize by rote, whether the subject is training a model or designing a relational schema.

Given his broader background in AI and machine learning, with a particular focus on training models on datasets that carry genuine real-world significance, often medical datasets prized for their precision and accuracy, it made sense that even a course as structurally different as Database Systems benefited from the same rigor and clarity he brought to Programming Fundamentals.

Adjusting to the summer semester schedule took some getting used to on its own, separate from the subject matter itself. The heat made the campus feel different in a tangible, physical way, and the overlapping presence of Ramadan meant that the daily rhythm of eating, studying, and attending class shifted considerably for a large portion of the class. Lectures that would normally happen at a certain energy level took on a different pace altogether, and there was a kind of unspoken understanding among both students and faculty that the usual intensity of the semester needed to bend slightly to accommodate the realities of fasting, family time around Eid, and the general slowdown that comes with a summer term squeezed in between two regular semesters.

Despite all of that, the actual content of Database Systems held its own appeal. Where Programming Fundamentals had been about teaching a machine to follow a sequence of logical steps, Database Systems asked a different kind of question entirely: how do you organize information so that it stays accurate, retrievable, and useful no matter how large or complex it grows? Concepts like normalization, primary and foreign keys, and entity-relationship modeling weren't just abstract definitions to memorize. They were practical answers to a very real problem: how do you design a system that doesn't fall apart under its own complexity once real data starts flowing through it.

Working through MySQL Workbench and WAMP Server in those early weeks also gave the course a hands-on quality similar to what we'd experienced with Python in Programming Fundamentals. Reading about database design in a textbook is one thing. Actually creating tables, writing queries against them, and watching the results come back in real time is what made the concepts stick. That pattern, theory followed immediately by hands-on practice, had clearly worked well in PF, and Dr. Bilal carried it over into this course just as deliberately.

This semester would go on to bring its own distinct set of challenges, many of them tied directly to the unusual circumstances of studying through Ramadan, a shift to online classes, and a second round of mid-terms. But that part of the story comes next.

Stepping back for a moment, I think there's something worth noting about what it means to take a second course with the same instructor after already having gone through one with them. There's a kind of shorthand that develops. I already knew roughly how Dr. Bilal structured a syllabus, how he sequenced difficulty across the semester, and what his lab evaluations would expect from us. That familiarity removed a layer of uncertainty that had been present throughout all of Programming Fundamentals, freeing up more mental energy to focus on the actual content of Database Systems rather than also having to figure out the unwritten rules of the classroom itself.

That carried-over trust also changed how I approached the early difficulties of a brand-new subject. Where Programming Fundamentals had required me to simultaneously learn the material and learn how university coursework worked at all, Database Systems only asked me to learn the material, since the surrounding structure was already familiar. That difference made the second course feel less daunting from the very first lecture, even before we'd covered anything technically demanding.
