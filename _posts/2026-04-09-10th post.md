Toward the end of Ramadan, our Database Systems classes shifted online, and that transition brought a new set of responsibilities, especially in my role as Class Representative. Managing an online class involves a different kind of coordination than a physical one. I was responsible for communicating class timings to everyone, collecting files and assignments from the whole class, and making sure everything reached Dr. Bilal in an organized way. It sounds simple in description, but in practice it meant a steady stream of small coordination tasks layered on top of my own coursework.

One particular memory from this period stands out. On a couple of occasions, Dr. Bilal had to take the class while traveling. The audio quality during those sessions wasn't great, sometimes barely audible, but the class still went ahead anyway. Everyone made an effort to follow along despite the technical limitations, and looking back, there's something admirable about an instructor pushing through travel logistics just to keep the course on schedule rather than simply canceling. That said, I'll be honest: a physical classroom session is simply a different experience from an online one, and no amount of effort on either side fully closes that gap.

Shortly after the shift to online classes, Eid-ul-Fitr arrived, and almost immediately afterward, our mid-term exams were upon us. The mid-term syllabus centered on MySQL queries, ER diagrams, and database keys, the same foundational topics we'd been building since the start of the semester. Given everything going on around that period, the disrupted routine of Ramadan, the online class transition, the general unpredictability of those weeks, my preparation for this mid-term wasn't as thorough as I would have liked under normal circumstances.

Despite that, my results came back better than I expected, enough that I was genuinely surprised when I saw my own marks. Curious about it, I asked Dr. Bilal directly, and he explained that the checking for this particular mid-term had been somewhat more lenient than usual. His reasoning was straightforward and, honestly, considerate: a lot of students were stressed about their papers given the circumstances of the semester, and he wanted to make sure everyone got a fair benefit of the doubt without anyone's confidence or self-esteem taking an unnecessary hit. It's a small detail, but it says something about an instructor's awareness of what students are actually going through beyond just the syllabus.

Being CR during this stretch meant I had a slightly closer view of how widespread that stress actually was across the class. Coordinating file submissions and assignment collection meant fielding messages from classmates who were clearly anxious about how the disrupted routine was affecting their preparation, on top of the usual logistical questions about timing and deadlines. It gave me a sense that the leniency Dr. Bilal mentioned wasn't an arbitrary decision made in isolation. It was a response to something he could likely see across the class as a whole, even without every student saying it outright.

The online classes themselves also forced a kind of self-discipline that physical classes don't really demand in the same way. When you're sitting in a real classroom, the environment itself does some of the work of keeping you focused. Online, especially with inconsistent audio and the general distractions of attending class from a hostel room, staying engaged required a more deliberate effort. I noticed myself having to consciously resist the temptation to multitask during lectures, something that simply isn't as tempting when you're physically present in a room with a professor and your classmates around you.

Looking back at this period as a whole, I think it was a useful preview of a kind of flexibility that the rest of university, and frankly the rest of any career, eventually demands: working through degraded conditions without letting the degraded conditions become an excuse for degraded effort. The audio wasn't great. The routine was disrupted. The preparation time was less than ideal. And yet the semester kept moving, the mid-term happened, and the class came out the other side of it intact.

This stretch of the semester, juggling online classes, Ramadan's shifted routines, CR responsibilities, and a mid-term under less-than-ideal preparation conditions, ended up being a good test of adaptability. University doesn't always hand you ideal circumstances to perform in, and learning to manage coursework and responsibility even when the conditions aren't perfect turned out to be its own kind of lesson, separate from anything in the Database Systems syllabus itself.

Looking back at this entire period now, I think what stands out most isn't any single difficulty I had to push through, but the accumulation of several at once: a disrupted routine, a sudden shift in class format, an increase in CR responsibilities, and an exam I felt underprepared for, all arriving within the same few weeks. Individually, none of these would have been particularly hard to manage. Together, they tested something closer to overall resilience than any single skill, and getting through that stretch intact gave me a clearer sense of how much I could actually handle when circumstances stopped being convenient.

If there's one thing I'd want a younger version of myself, or any student facing a similarly chaotic stretch, to know, it's that the individual pieces always feel more manageable in hindsight than they do while you're inside them. At the time, online classes, Ramadan's disrupted routine, and an underprepared mid-term felt like they were colliding all at once with no clear way through. Looking back, none of it was as unmanageable as it felt in the moment, and the semester kept moving forward regardless.
