After the mid-term exams, the second half of my first semester took a turn I genuinely hadn't expected. Our Programming Fundamentals final project shifted into territory that felt completely different from anything we'd done before: Machine Learning. Using Python, we started learning how to actually train models, and with that came an entirely new vocabulary of concepts. Data loading, data visualization, and the broader idea of how a model "learns" from data all started entering my understanding for the first time.

One of the more useful conceptual distinctions we picked up during this stretch was the difference between classification and regression models. Understanding when a problem calls for one versus the other, and why that distinction matters for how you structure your data and your approach, was the kind of foundational knowledge that made everything afterward easier to grasp. This wasn't just an academic exercise either. This same conceptual foundation would directly carry over into our Programming Fundamentals final project, which made the timing of this introduction feel intentional rather than incidental.

Dr. Bilal handled this transition the same way he'd handled everything else throughout the semester: by starting from the basics and building up methodically, explaining each new concept in enough detail that confusion didn't have room to creep in. Given that machine learning was an entirely new subject for most of us, that approach mattered enormously. It's easy to get lost in unfamiliar terminology when a topic is introduced too quickly, but that wasn't the experience here.

What I genuinely appreciated about this part of the semester was how different it felt from everything I'd studied before. Machine Learning had a kind of novelty to it that made the effort of learning it feel less like an obligation and more like genuine curiosity. That said, I won't pretend it was easy from the start. Like most new subjects, the initial exposure felt difficult, almost overwhelming in places. But the same pattern that had played out with hostel life, with Programming Fundamentals itself, and with adjusting to university more broadly repeated itself here too: things that feel hard in the beginning get steadily more manageable with consistent exposure and effort.

By this point in the semester, I'd also reached a kind of overall stability that made absorbing a new subject like this easier than it would have been a few months earlier. My hostel routine felt normal rather than foreign. My understanding of how university coursework operated had matured. Even my responsibilities as CR had become second nature rather than a source of stress. All of that stability created the right conditions for tackling something as conceptually dense as machine learning without it feeling like one challenge too many.

Looking back, I think the timing of this introduction mattered more than I realized at the time. If machine learning had been thrown at us during those first chaotic weeks of hostel adjustment and unfamiliar classrooms, I doubt it would have landed the same way. Learning a genuinely new and conceptually different subject requires a certain amount of mental bandwidth, and that bandwidth simply wasn't available to me in September. By the time Dr. Bilal introduced data loading, visualization, and the classification-versus-regression distinction, I had enough free mental space to actually sit with the material instead of just trying to survive the week.

I also noticed something about my own learning style during this stretch that I hadn't fully recognized before: I tend to understand a new technical subject best when I can see it applied to something concrete almost immediately. Abstract explanations of what a model "learns" from data didn't fully click for me until we started working with actual numbers, actual columns of data, and actual visualizations that showed patterns I could point to. Once that visual, hands-on layer was added to the theory, the abstract concepts started to make a lot more sense. I suspect this is true for a lot of students, which is probably why Dr. Bilal paired every new concept with a practical walkthrough rather than leaving it purely theoretical.

There was also a small but important shift in how I thought about programming itself during this period. Up until this point, Programming Fundamentals had largely meant writing code that solved a defined problem with a defined, predictable output. Machine learning introduced a different kind of thinking: writing code whose output depends on patterns in data rather than a fixed set of rules I'd written myself. That distinction, between deterministic programming and data-driven modeling, was a genuinely new mental model for me, and getting comfortable with it took a bit of unlearning alongside the new learning.

By the end of this stretch, machine learning had gone from being an unfamiliar, slightly intimidating term I'd heard mentioned in passing to something I could actually describe with some confidence: loading a dataset, visualizing it to understand its shape, deciding whether the problem in front of me called for classification or regression, and then training a model against it. That progression, from vague unfamiliarity to working understanding, happened faster than I expected, and a large part of that speed came down to how deliberately Dr. Bilal sequenced the material.

This stretch of the semester ended up being a turning point in a way I didn't fully appreciate until later. The machine learning fundamentals we picked up here weren't just a one-off topic. They became the backbone of our entire final project, the first real, substantial piece of technical work I'd complete in my engineering degree, and a foundation I'd keep returning to well beyond this single course.

Looking back on this period now, after having gone through an entire additional semester since, I can see how much of my later confidence with technical material traces back to this specific stretch of learning. It was the first time I genuinely enjoyed the process of struggling with something difficult, rather than just tolerating the struggle as a necessary cost of getting a grade. That distinction, between enduring difficulty and actually being curious enough to push through it, is one I didn't have language for at the time, but it's exactly what made this introduction to machine learning feel different from every other subject I'd encountered up to that point in my education.
